SPOT: Selection Power Over Time

Estimates the power to detect selection at one candidate locus from allele frequencies sampled at two or more points in time. It accounts for genetic drift, migration, changes in population size, haploid, diploid or haplodiploid inheritance, the sex and relatedness of the sampled individuals, and genotype calls from low-coverage sequencing. Every change of a setting reruns all simulations in the browser.

Model: one locus with two alleles, discrete generations, separate numbers of breeding females and males (or a single haploid population), migrants from a source population, and selection that can differ between the sexes. Power is the proportion of simulations under selection in which selection is detected. The detection threshold is set from neutral simulations (no selection) that contain the same drift, migration, sampling, relatedness and genotype-call errors as the study design, so it is not fooled by them. The Method section below and the README explain every setting.

Why two thresholds. A standard test assumes that the random choice of independent individuals is the only source of chance. Z then follows a standard normal distribution without selection, and the threshold is a fixed number, the sampling-only threshold: 1.645 for a one-sided test at 5% (z₁₋α′ in general). Drift, siblings in the sample and genotype calls at low or unequal depth make Z vary more than this, so the fixed threshold is too low and finds "selection" where there is none. The drift-aware threshold is read from the neutral simulations, which include these sources of chance. If power stays low whatever the sample size, drift is the limit, not the number of individuals: the sample-size planner below shows this.

Method

How power is computed: technical details

The usual calculation, without drift

A common back-of-envelope calculation of power ignores drift1. The allele frequency goes deterministically from p₀ at the first date to pt at the last, and the only source of chance is the sampling of n₁ gene copies at the first date and n₂ at the last. Power is then the probability that the test rejects, added up over all the counts the two samples can give:

Power=∑x=0n1∑y=0n2P(X=x)P(Y=y)𝟙[Z(x,y)>z1−α′]X∼Binomial(n1,p0),Y∼Binomial(n2,pt)

Here x and y are the numbers of copies of the favoured allele in the first and last samples, and P(X = x) and P(Y = y) are their binomial probabilities. The term 𝟙[…] equals 1 when the score Z computed from x and y (see "The score Z") exceeds the threshold z1−α′, that is, when the test detects the change, and 0 otherwise. The sum therefore adds up the probabilities of all the pairs of sample counts that lead to a detection. The example "Analytic baseline" (no drift, perfect genotypes) reproduces this setting, with the threshold read from simulations instead of the normal approximation.

The score Z

Both tests use the Cochran–Armitage test for trend2,3 on counts of gene copies. At sampling date i (time ti, in generations), xi of the ni called gene copies carry the favoured allele:

Z=∑ixi(ti−t‾)p‾(1−p‾)∑ini(ti−t‾)2t‾=∑initi∑ini,p‾=∑ixi∑ini

Here t̄ is the mean sampling time, weighted by the number of gene copies at each date, and p̄ is the frequency of the favoured allele in all dates pooled. The numerator is positive when the favoured allele is more common at later dates than at earlier ones. The denominator is the standard deviation the numerator would have if the random sampling of gene copies were the only source of chance. The trend test uses all dates: it fits a regression line of allele frequency on time through all dates (least squares, each date weighted by its number of gene copies) and tests whether its slope is positive. The first-versus-last test uses only the first and last dates; with two dates the line simply joins the two frequencies, and the test is the usual comparison of two proportions. Both are one-sided: they test for an increase of the favoured allele.

When sampling is the only source of chance and enough copies are sampled, Z follows approximately a standard normal distribution: this is what the sampling-only threshold assumes. Drift, siblings in the sample and genotype-call errors make Z vary more, and with few copies of a rare allele Z takes only a few values. In both cases the real distribution of Z without selection differs from the standard normal, which is why SPOT reads the threshold from neutral simulations.

What power means and how SPOT computes it

Power is the probability that a sampling design detects selection of the chosen strength when it is acting. A power of 80% means that this selection is detected in 8 cases out of 10 and missed in 2. SPOT estimates it in six steps.

  1. Simulate. SPOT simulates a population from the first to the last sampling date R times with selection on the favoured allele (selection simulations; R = "Selection simulations", 1,000 by default) and at least 2R times without selection (neutral simulations; more when many loci are tested, so that about 10 neutral simulations lie beyond the threshold). Both start at the same p₀ and share the same Ne, migration and history, so drift (unless switched off) acts on both. With 1,000 selection simulations, power varies by about 2 percentage points from one run to the next; for a final estimate, we recommend 5,000.
  2. Sample. At each sampling date, the chosen number of individuals is drawn from the population, as groups of siblings if chosen. A diploid individual (diploids, haplodiploid females) carries two gene copies; a haploid individual (haploids, haplodiploid males) carries one.
  3. Sequence and call genotypes. Each individual receives a number of reads drawn from a Poisson distribution with the mean depth of its date, and each read shows the wrong allele with the per-read error rate of its date (earlier points or last point). Individuals with fewer reads than the minimum depth are left out. Diploid genotypes are called by maximum likelihood7, haploid ones by the majority of reads (ties at random).
  4. Score. From the called gene copies at each date, SPOT computes for every simulation the score Z (see "The score Z"): how fast the frequency of the favoured allele rises over time, relative to how much it would vary if the random choice of gene copies were the only source of chance. Z close to 0 means no clear change; a large Z means a clear rise.
  5. Set the threshold. In the neutral simulations, the allele is not selected, but Z still varies by chance: drift, the random choice of individuals, siblings in the sample and genotype errors. The drift-aware threshold is the value of Z exceeded by only a proportion α′ of the neutral simulations (α′ = α, the significance level, divided by the number L of candidate loci). A larger Z is then unlikely to arise by chance. Neutral simulations exactly at the threshold count for a fraction, so that this proportion is exactly α′. If too few neutral simulations lie beyond it, the threshold is extrapolated from a normal distribution fitted to the neutral Z values, which can be far off for a rare allele.
  6. Count. Power is the proportion of selection simulations whose Z exceeds the threshold (same rule for values at the threshold). The sampling-only threshold is z1−α′, the value exceeded with probability α′ by a standard normal variable (1.645 for α′ = 5%); its false-positive rate is the proportion of neutral simulations above it.

What moves the power

FactorMechanismEffect on power
Individuals (sampling design)Each diploid individual adds 2 gene copies, so the sampling variance of the frequency at a point is about p(1−p)/2n (haploid individuals: 1 copy, p(1−p)/n). The last point usually counts most because the change is largest there.More power, with diminishing returns once drift dominates.
Intermediate points (sampling design)The trend test uses the points between the first and the last. These help most when selection acts steadily from early on. If selection starts after the last intermediate point, they add little to detecting change, although they still help date it.Usually a modest gain in power.
Selection starts in yearThe number of generations under selection sets the size of Δp. Samples taken before onset only measure the baseline.Later onset gives less power.
Breeding females and males per generation (Ne)Drift widens the neutral distribution and raises the drift-aware threshold. Rare favoured alleles are also often lost before selection can act.Small Ne means much less power and, unless the allele is rare, a strongly inflated false-positive rate for the sampling-only threshold.
Inheritance system: haplodiploidHaploid males express every allele, so even recessive favoured alleles are exposed to selection. Ne = 9NfNm/(4Nm+2Nf)6 instead of 4NfNm/(Nf+Nm)4.More power than in diploids for recessive alleles, which rise much faster; about the same for dominant alleles.
Dominance in diploid individualsRare recessive alleles are almost invisible to selection in diploid individuals.From a low p₀, most power for dominant alleles and least for recessive ones.
Selection in males (× s)In haplodiploids, males carry one third of the gene copies and pass their single copy to all their daughters. Because selection on haploid males is fully exposed, it contributes about half of the response for additive alleles, and most of it for recessive alleles.Setting it to 0 (a trait not expressed in males) slows the response and lowers power.
Mean depth, per-read error rates, minimum depthAt low depth, heterozygotes are often called homozygous (allelic dropout), and individuals below the minimum depth drop out. Both add noise and reduce the effective sample size. With a high minimum depth, a low mean depth mostly removes individuals; with a minimum depth of 1, it keeps individuals with poorly called genotypes.Power falls below about 4–6×, where missing calls and heterozygote dropout rise steeply; above about 10×, more depth adds little power.
Males in the sample (haplodiploid)Among haploid males with enough reads, the single allele is called almost perfectly even at 2–3×, because there is no heterozygote to miss; but at that depth many males fall below the minimum depth and are dropped. His allele comes from his mother, so the male frequency reflects the female population after selection on males.Up to about 6×, about the same power per individual as diploid females: one gene copy instead of two, but called almost without error. At higher depth, where female genotypes are also called well, females give slightly more power.
Sampled individuals per familyIndividuals of the same family carry correlated alleles, so several siblings give less information than the same number of unrelated individuals.A smaller effective sample size, giving lower power and more false positives with the sampling-only threshold.
Inheritance system: haploidEvery allele is exposed to selection, as in haplodiploid males, and each sampled individual carries one gene copy. Ne equals the number of breeding individuals.More power from the fast response, even from a low frequency; less power from carrying half as many gene copies per sampled individual as a diploid.
Set Ne separately for each intervalNe is held at a chosen value between two sampling points, for example a bottleneck or an expansion between two dates. Drift is strongest in the intervals with low Ne.A short, severe bottleneck can cost more power than a long, mild decline; an interval with a larger Ne has less drift and gives slightly more power.
Ne at the last point relative to the firstNe changes exponentially to the chosen ratio by the last point: below 1 (decline), drift speeds up late in the series; above 1 (increase), it slows down.A decline lowers power and increases the genetic diversity lost; an increase does the opposite, with smaller gains once drift is already weak.
Migrants per generation, mEach generation, a fraction m of the breeding adults is replaced by migrants from a source population whose allele frequency stays fixed.Lower power when migrants carry the starting frequency p₀: they pull the frequency back towards it. A source where the allele is fixed mimics selection. Migration also slows the loss of genetic diversity.
Candidate loci tested, LThe per-locus significance level becomes α′ = α/L, which needs a larger Z to reject.Lower power, especially when the effect is marginal.
Years per generationFewer years per generation means more generations between the sampling dates, so more time for both selection and drift. Long-lived species go through few generations even over centuries.Shorter generations: larger Δp and more power, until drift over the many generations loses or fixes the allele; power then falls.
